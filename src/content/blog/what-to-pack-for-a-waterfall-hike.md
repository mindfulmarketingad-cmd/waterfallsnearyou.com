Waterfall hikes have a reputation as easy outings, and many of them are short. But they come with a specific set of conditions that catch people unprepared: wet and slippery rock, creek crossings, spray that soaks clothing, cool air in shaded gorges, and steep stair-step descents into canyons with no cell signal. Packing for a waterfall hike is less about carrying a lot and more about carrying the right things. This checklist covers what to bring for a typical day hike to a waterfall, why each item matters, and how to adjust for season and distance. Whether you are visiting a famous falls or tracking down lesser-known waterfalls near you, a well-packed bag makes the trip safer and more comfortable.

## The Quick Checklist

If you want the short version, here it is. The sections below explain each category.

| Category | Essentials | Nice to have |
|---|---|---|
| Footwear | Hiking shoes or boots with lugged soles | Gaiters, water shoes, traction devices in winter |
| Clothing | Moisture-wicking base layer, insulating layer, rain jacket | Spare socks, warm hat, gloves |
| Navigation | Offline map, saved coordinates, charged phone | Paper topographic map, compass, GPS device |
| Hydration and food | Water, filter or treatment, snacks | Electrolyte mix, lunch, thermos in winter |
| Safety | First aid kit, headlamp, whistle, emergency layer | Satellite messenger, trekking poles |
| Sun and bugs | Sunscreen, insect repellent | Sun hat, sunglasses |
| Water and spray | Dry bag or zip bags for electronics | Small towel, pack cover |
| Leave No Trace | Trash bag | Trowel and waste bags where required |

## Footwear: The Most Important Choice

More waterfall trips go wrong because of footwear than any other gear decision. Trails near falls are often wet, muddy, rooty, or rocky, with stretches of stone steps that stay damp all year.

### What to look for

- **Lugged rubber outsoles** with deep tread to grip mud and loose ground.
- **Sticky rubber compounds** that perform better on wet rock than hard plastic soles.
- **Good fit and support,** especially on steep descents into gorges.
- **Waterproof or quick-drying construction.** Waterproof boots keep feet dry through mud and shallow puddles; breathable, non-waterproof shoes dry faster after a crossing. Both approaches work; choose based on season and terrain.

No sole grips reliably on algae-covered rock near the falls, so good shoes are not a license to climb on wet ledges. Our [waterfall safety tips](/blog/waterfall-safety-tips) explain why those surfaces are so treacherous.

### Optional footwear

- **Water shoes** with closed toes are useful if you plan to wade where it is calm and permitted. They protect against sharp rocks and broken glass.
- **Gaiters** keep mud and water out of your boots on wet trails.
- **Traction devices** such as microspikes slip over boots and make icy trails far safer in winter.

## Clothing and Layers

Waterfalls create their own microclimate. Gorges are shaded and cool, spray soaks anything nearby, and a breeze off falling water can feel surprisingly cold, even in summer.

### The layering system

1. **Base layer.** A moisture-wicking synthetic or wool shirt. Avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold.
2. **Insulating layer.** A fleece or light puffy jacket for rest stops and cool gorges.
3. **Shell.** A rain jacket that also blocks spray and wind. On many waterfall trails, this is the most useful layer you carry.

### Extras worth packing

- **Spare socks** in a zip bag. Dry socks after a creek crossing or a muddy section make the hike out far more pleasant.
- **Warm hat and light gloves** in spring, fall, and winter, or at higher elevations year-round.
- **Quick-drying hiking pants or shorts** rather than jeans.

## Navigation: Offline Maps and Coordinates

Many waterfalls sit at the bottom of valleys, deep in gorges, or far from towns, which means cell service is often weak or nonexistent. Do not count on loading a map once you are on the trail.

### Before you leave

- **Download offline maps** of the area in your hiking or mapping app. Make sure the download covers the full route, the trailhead, and some surrounding area.
- **Save the waterfall's coordinates.** Every listing on this site includes the latitude and longitude of the falls, which you can save as a waypoint or pin in your mapping app.
- **Note the USGS topographic quadrangle name.** Each listing also shows the 7.5-minute quad the waterfall falls within. That makes it easy to look up the detailed topographic map for the area, which shows contour lines, streams, and terrain.
- **Read the trail description.** Know the distance, elevation change, and any junctions.
- **Charge your phone fully,** and consider bringing a small battery pack.

### On the trail

- **Carry a paper map** of the area as a backup, along with a basic compass if you know how to use one.
- **Track your progress** occasionally so you know where you are if you need to turn back or call for help.
- **Use the coordinates** if you need to describe your location to rescuers.

## Water and Food

Even a short hike can be thirsty work, especially when the return trip climbs out of a gorge.

- **Carry enough water.** About half a liter per hour of moderate hiking is a common starting point; carry more on hot days or steep trails.
- **Treat stream water.** Clear mountain water can still carry pathogens. Use a filter, chemical treatment, or UV purifier.
- **Bring snacks.** Salty, energy-dense foods like nuts, trail mix, and bars keep energy up.
- **Add electrolytes** on hot days or longer hikes.
- **Pack out all food waste,** including fruit peels and shells.

## First Aid and Safety Gear

A basic kit handles most minor trail problems and buys time in a real emergency.

### A practical day-hike first aid kit

- Adhesive bandages in several sizes
- Blister treatment, such as moleskin or blister pads
- Gauze pads and medical tape
- Elastic bandage for sprains
- Antiseptic wipes
- Tweezers for splinters and ticks
- Pain relievers and antihistamines
- Any personal medications

### Other safety items

- **Headlamp** with fresh batteries. Many hikers underestimate how long it takes to climb out of a canyon, and days are short in fall and winter.
- **Whistle.** Three blasts is a widely recognized distress signal, and a whistle carries farther than a voice over the roar of falling water.
- **Emergency blanket or bivy.** Lightweight and valuable if you are stuck waiting for help, especially when wet.
- **Trekking poles.** They add stability on slick descents and creek crossings, and take strain off your knees on steps.
- **Knife or multi-tool.**
- **Satellite messenger or personal locator beacon** for remote trails without cell coverage.

## Protecting Electronics From Water

Spray, rain, and creek crossings are hard on phones and cameras.

- **Use a dry bag or zip-top bags** for your phone, battery pack, and car keys.
- **Bring a microfiber cloth** to wipe lenses and screens.
- **Use a pack cover** or line your pack with a trash bag in wet weather.

## Sun, Bugs, and Comfort

- **Sunscreen and sunglasses** for exposed approach trails, even if the falls themselves are shaded.
- **Insect repellent.** Damp areas near streams can be buggy, and ticks are common in many regions.
- **Sun hat or cap.**
- **Small towel** if you plan to wade.
- **Sit pad** for a comfortable rest stop on wet ground.

## Packing for the Season

Conditions change a lot throughout the year. Adjust your kit to match. Our guide to [the best time to visit waterfalls](/blog/best-time-to-visit-waterfalls) covers what to expect from each season.

### Spring

High water and mud are common. Add gaiters, extra socks, and a reliable rain jacket. Be prepared to turn back at high creek crossings.

### Summer

Carry extra water, sunscreen, and bug spray. Watch for afternoon thunderstorms and pack your rain shell even on clear mornings.

### Fall

Bring an extra warm layer and a headlamp for shorter days. Wet leaves on rocks are slick, so traction matters.

### Winter

Add traction devices, warm gloves, an insulated jacket, and a thermos of something hot. Plan for icy steps and the possibility of falling ice near cliffs.

## Packing for Kids and Dogs

Hiking with family changes the packing list.

- **For kids:** extra snacks, a full change of clothes, a small first aid kit tailored to children, and a properly fitted life jacket if they will be near water. Our guide to [waterfall hikes with kids](/blog/waterfall-hikes-with-kids) has more ideas for keeping young hikers safe and happy.
- **For dogs:** a leash, water and a bowl, waste bags, and a towel. Check whether dogs are allowed; rules vary by park and agency.

## Leave No Trace Essentials

A small trash bag is one of the most useful items you can carry. Pack out everything you bring in, and consider picking up litter left by others. Where facilities are not available, follow local rules for human waste, which may require a trowel or waste bags. Our guide to [Leave No Trace at waterfalls](/blog/leave-no-trace-at-waterfalls) covers how to protect these fragile places.

## Final Check Before You Leave

Before you head out the door:

1. Confirm the trail is open and check recent conditions with the managing agency.
2. Check the weather forecast for the area, including upstream.
3. Download offline maps and save the waterfall's coordinates.
4. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return.
5. Run through the checklist above.
