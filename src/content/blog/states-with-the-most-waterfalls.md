Ask a group of hikers which state has the most waterfalls and you will hear confident answers for Oregon, Washington, North Carolina, New York and a half-dozen others. The honest answer depends on how you count. For this analysis we used the most consistent nationwide source available: the U.S. Geological Survey's Geographic Names Information System (GNIS), the federal government's official database of place names. We counted every feature classified as "Falls" in each state using our October 2026 download of the data. The result is a ranking of the states with the most officially named waterfalls, along with a look at the geology, glaciation, rainfall and mountain ranges behind the numbers, and an important caveat about what these counts do and do not mean.

## How We Counted

GNIS assigns every named feature a feature class, such as Stream, Summit, Lake or Falls. The Falls class covers named waterfalls, cascades and similar features. Our method was simple:

- **Source:** USGS GNIS domestic names, downloaded October 2026.
- **Filter:** Features with the feature class "Falls."
- **Grouping:** Counted by the state recorded for each feature.
- **Scope:** All 50 states, the District of Columbia and U.S. territories.

This yielded about 2,530 named falls in total: 2,516 across the 50 states and the District of Columbia, plus 17 in U.S. territories (Guam 7, Puerto Rico 5 and American Samoa 5). Delaware has no features in the Falls class.

These same records power WaterfallsNearYou.com, which lists more than 2,500 named waterfalls organized by state and nearest city, each with coordinates, county, the USGS 7.5-minute topographic quadrangle name, nearby waterfalls, and map and directions links.

## Why Some States Have So Many Named Waterfalls

Waterfalls form where streams meet an abrupt drop, usually because of contrasts in rock hardness, uplift, faulting, volcanic layering or glacial carving. (Our guide to [types of waterfalls](/blog/types-of-waterfalls) explains these processes in detail.) The states at the top of the list combine several of these factors.

### Mountain Ranges and Topography

Relief is the foundation. Waterfalls need elevation to lose, and steep terrain concentrates that loss into short distances. The Cascade Range runs through Oregon, Washington and Northern California; the Sierra Nevada and Coast Ranges cover much of California; the northern Rockies span Montana, Wyoming, Idaho and Colorado; and the Appalachians stretch from Maine through Pennsylvania and New York to North Carolina, Tennessee and Alabama. Of the top 15 states, only Michigan and Hawaii fall outside these major ranges, and both owe their counts to other factors covered below.

Grouping the data by region makes the mountain pattern clear:

The five Northern Plains states listed here have 20 named falls combined, fewer than Pennsylvania alone, while Oregon and Washington together have 423.

### Volcanic Rock and Rainfall in the Pacific Northwest

Oregon and Washington pair high relief with abundant precipitation, especially west of the Cascades. Thick sequences of layered basalt from ancient lava flows create the hard ledges that make tall, sheer waterfalls, and the Columbia River Gorge along their shared border is famous for falls dropping off basalt cliffs that were scoured by massive Ice Age floods. Add glacially carved valleys in the Cascades and Olympics, and the region has nearly every ingredient for waterfalls.

### Glaciation in the North

Much of the northern United States was covered by ice sheets during the last ice age. Glaciers scraped away soil, exposed bedrock ledges, deepened valleys and rearranged drainage, leaving streams flowing over abrupt steps that they have not yet worn smooth. That glacial legacy helps explain the strong showing of New York, Michigan, Maine, New Hampshire, Wisconsin and Minnesota.

New York combines glaciated terrain with layered sedimentary rock: streams cutting through alternating hard and soft layers produce many ledges and gorges, particularly in the Finger Lakes region and around the Adirondacks and Catskills. Michigan's count is driven heavily by the Upper Peninsula, where streams flow over resistant ancient rock toward Lake Superior.

### Hanging Valleys in the West

In California, Montana, Wyoming, Idaho and Colorado, mountain glaciers carved deep main valleys and left tributary valleys hanging above them. Streams spilling out of those hanging valleys create some of the tallest falls in the country, with Yosemite Valley the best-known example. Snowmelt-fed flow means many of these falls are strongest in late spring and early summer; our guide to the [best time to visit waterfalls](/blog/best-time-to-visit-waterfalls) covers seasonal timing.

### Escarpments and Rainfall in the Southern Appalachians

North Carolina ranks eighth, and the surrounding southern Appalachian states add many more. The Blue Ridge Escarpment, where the mountains drop sharply toward the Piedmont, concentrates waterfalls along a steep boundary, and the region receives some of the highest rainfall in the eastern United States. Tennessee's count benefits from both the Appalachians and the Cumberland Plateau, where streams fall off the edge of resistant sandstone caprock.

### Islands, Rain and Young Terrain in Hawaii

Hawaii has only a small land area yet ranks ninth with 99 named falls. Steep volcanic slopes and extremely high rainfall on windward sides of the islands produce many falls, often tall and ribbon-like, dropping into deeply eroded valleys.

### Why Flat States Have So Few

States at the bottom of the list mostly share low relief. The Great Plains and much of the Gulf and Atlantic Coastal Plains are broad and gently sloping, and streams there often flow over soft sediments that do not form persistent ledges. Florida's porous limestone and flat terrain leave little opportunity for falls. Where waterfalls do appear in these states, they tend to be small and localized.

## The Big Caveat: Named Is Not the Same as Best

These rankings measure one specific thing: **how many waterfalls have official names in a federal database.** That is useful because it is consistent across the country, but it has real limits.

- **Many waterfalls have no official name.** Countless falls, especially small or remote ones, have never been named in GNIS. Some states with heavy recreation or detailed historical surveys may have more falls named than states with similar terrain.
- **Local names may not be recorded.** A waterfall that everyone in town calls by a nickname may not appear in GNIS at all, or may be listed under a different name.
- **Naming history varies.** Which features got names depended on settlement patterns, mapping projects, local custom and naming decisions over more than a century.
- **Feature classification is not perfect.** Some features classified as Falls are small cascades or rapids, while some notable waterfalls may be recorded under another class or as part of a stream.
- **Counts say nothing about quality.** Size, flow, beauty, accessibility and crowds are not captured. A state with fewer named falls may have spectacular ones.
- **Area is not adjusted.** Large states naturally have more room for named features. A per-area comparison would tell a different story, especially for smaller northeastern states.

In short, use this ranking as a portrait of where named falls are concentrated and why, not as a verdict on which state has the "best" waterfalls.
